The Audit-Ready Maintenance Checklist for Manufacturers

A practical guide to what auditors, inspectors, and insurers actually ask for — and how to make sure you can produce it without the scramble.

Before You Start

Most manufacturing maintenance teams are not failing to do the work. They are failing to prove it happened, quickly, in a format someone else will accept. This checklist covers the records that come up most often in manufacturing audits, HSE inspections, and insurance reviews, so you can check, in advance, whether you would be ready today.

A note on the regulations below: intervals and requirements depend on your specific equipment, site risk, and a competent person's assessment. This is a practical starting checklist, not legal advice. Always confirm specifics with your compliance advisor or a qualified inspector.

Section 1: Asset & Equipment Records

Can you produce, for any critical asset:

  • A current list of equipment, including location and identifying details
  • Service/maintenance history: what was done, and when
  • Manufacturer-recommended service intervals, and whether they have been followed
  • Records of any breakdowns or failures, and what was done to resolve them
Why it matters: Auditors and insurers frequently ask "when was this last serviced?" If the answer takes hours to find, that is a red flag regardless of whether the work was actually done.

Section 2: Preventive Maintenance (PM) Records

  • A documented PM schedule for critical/high-risk equipment
  • Evidence that scheduled PMs were actually completed, not just planned
  • Records of who completed each PM, and when
  • A way to show overdue or missed PMs, and what happened as a result
Why it matters: Having a schedule is not enough. Auditors want to see that the schedule was followed, or that exceptions were handled properly.

Section 3: Machine Guarding & Work Equipment (PUWER)

  • A risk assessment for each piece of work equipment, covering guarding, isolation, and controls
  • Evidence that guards, emergency stops, and interlocks are inspected and maintained, not just installed
  • Records of visual and thorough inspections, carried out by a competent person
  • A process for handling equipment that fails inspection: taken out of service, not just noted
Why it matters: PUWER covers virtually all workplace equipment, and inspectors expect to see ongoing maintenance evidence, not just a one-time installation record.

Section 4: Lifting Equipment (LOLER)

  • A register of all lifting equipment on site: cranes, hoists, forklifts, slings, chains
  • Records of thorough examinations by a competent person, at the required interval
  • Safe working load (SWL) marking confirmed for each item
  • A process for reporting and closing out defects found during examination
Why it matters: Lifting equipment carries some of the strictest inspection and recordkeeping requirements in manufacturing. Missing or incomplete LOLER records are a common inspection failure point.

Section 5: Pressure Systems (PSSR)

  • A written scheme of examination for pressure systems: boilers, compressors, storage tanks
  • Records of examinations carried out against that scheme
  • Evidence any identified defects were addressed before the system returned to use
Why it matters: Pressure system failures are high-consequence events. Inspectors and insurers treat gaps here as a serious red flag.

Section 6: Hazardous Substances & Local Exhaust Ventilation (COSHH)

  • An up-to-date inventory of hazardous substances used or stored on site
  • Safety data sheets accessible for each substance
  • Records of LEV (Local Exhaust Ventilation) system examinations
  • Health surveillance records where exposure risk requires it
Why it matters: COSHH compliance is frequently checked alongside general maintenance records, since LEV systems are maintenance assets in their own right.

Section 7: Electrical Safety (Fixed Wire Testing / EICR)

  • A current Electrical Installation Condition Report (EICR) for the site
  • Confirmation of the correct testing interval for your premises type. Industrial and high-risk environments typically need more frequent testing than general commercial space
  • Records of any remedial work identified and completed
  • PAT testing records for portable appliances, where applicable
Why it matters: Electrical faults are a leading cause of workplace fires, and insurers commonly request current EICR documentation before honoring claims.

Section 8: Fire Risk Assessments

  • A current fire risk assessment, with a clear date
  • Records of any actions identified in the assessment, and whether they were completed
  • A schedule for the next review
  • Evidence of fire safety equipment checks: extinguishers, alarms, emergency lighting
Why it matters: Fire risk assessments are one of the most commonly requested documents in inspections, and one of the easiest to lose track of if it is not tied to a system with reminders.

Section 9: Permits to Work

  • A record of any permits issued for higher-risk work: hot work, confined spaces, electrical isolation, work at height
  • Evidence of authorisation before work started, not after
  • Signatures from the people who approved and carried out the work
  • An accessible archive, not a paper folder that could go missing
Why it matters: Permit-to-work records are frequently requested after any incident, near-miss, or routine audit involving higher-risk maintenance activity.

Section 10: Checklists & Routine Inspections

  • Evidence that routine inspections and checks actually happened, not just that they were scheduled
  • Timestamped records, ideally with the name of who completed each check
  • A way to flag and track any issues found during an inspection
  • Proof the issue was resolved, not just identified
Why it matters: A missed inspection is a finding. A completed inspection with no record of it happening is treated the same way by most auditors.

The Real Test

Ask yourself honestly: if someone asked for any of the above right now, how long would it take you to produce it?

  • Minutes — you are in good shape
  • Hours — you are at risk, but recoverable
  • Days, or "I am not sure we have that" — this is the gap worth closing before it costs you

What Changes With a System Behind It

When maintenance work, PMs, LOLER/PUWER/PSSR examinations, COSHH/LEV records, electrical testing, fire risk assessments, permits, and inspections are all logged as they happen, tied to the asset, the person, and the date, producing any of the above becomes a report you can pull in minutes, not a project you have to reconstruct.

That is the difference between doing the work and being able to prove the work was done.
