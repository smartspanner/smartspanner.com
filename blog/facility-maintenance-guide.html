Facility maintenance covers everything required to keep buildings, infrastructure, and building systems in good working condition. It includes HVAC, electrical, plumbing, fire safety, structural upkeep, and grounds maintenance.

Effective facility maintenance protects the people who use the building, preserves asset value, ensures regulatory compliance, and controls operating costs. Poor maintenance leads to breakdowns, safety hazards, uncomfortable environments, and expensive emergency repairs.

This guide covers what facility maintenance involves, the different types, how to build a maintenance plan, and the best practices that keep facilities running smoothly.

What Is Facility Maintenance?

Facility maintenance is the process of maintaining and repairing the physical infrastructure of a building or site. It encompasses all the systems and components that keep a facility operational, safe, and comfortable for occupants.

The scope of facility maintenance typically includes:

  • HVAC systems (heating, ventilation, air conditioning)
  • Electrical systems (lighting, distribution, emergency power)
  • Plumbing and water systems
  • Fire protection systems (alarms, sprinklers, extinguishers)
  • Lifts and escalators
  • Building envelope (roof, walls, windows, doors)
  • Grounds and external areas (car parks, landscaping, drainage)
  • Security systems (CCTV, access control, intruder alarms)
  • Interior finishes (flooring, ceilings, paint, signage)

Facility maintenance is relevant to any organisation that occupies or manages physical space, from office buildings and retail centres to hospitals, schools, warehouses, and industrial sites.

The goal is not just to fix things when they break. It is to manage building assets proactively so they perform reliably, remain safe, and retain their value over time.

Types of Facility Maintenance

Understanding the different types of maintenance helps you build a balanced strategy. Most well-managed facilities use a combination of all four approaches, weighted according to the criticality and cost of each asset.

Reactive Maintenance

Also called breakdown or corrective maintenance, this is the fix-it-when-it-breaks approach. While some reactive maintenance is unavoidable, relying on it as your primary strategy is expensive and disruptive.

Reactive maintenance is appropriate for non-critical assets where the cost of failure is low and preventive maintenance would not be cost-effective. A lightbulb in a storage cupboard, for example, can be replaced when it fails without significant consequence.

However, applying a reactive approach to critical systems like HVAC, fire safety, or lifts is risky, expensive, and often non-compliant with regulations.

Preventive Maintenance

Maintenance performed on a scheduled basis to prevent failures before they occur. Tasks are triggered by time intervals (monthly filter changes) or usage thresholds (service after a set number of operating hours).

Preventive maintenance is the foundation of a good facility maintenance programme. It reduces unexpected failures, extends asset life, and provides predictable maintenance costs.

  • Scheduled inspections and servicing at regular intervals
  • Filter replacements, belt changes, and cleaning routines
  • Lubrication, calibration, and adjustment schedules
  • Seasonal preparation (winterisation, summer cooling system checks)
  • Annual testing and certification of safety systems

The challenge with preventive maintenance is determining the right frequency. Too frequent and you waste resources. Too infrequent and you risk failures between services. Manufacturer recommendations and historical data help calibrate the schedule.

Predictive Maintenance

Uses condition monitoring data to predict when equipment is likely to fail. Maintenance is performed based on actual equipment condition rather than fixed schedules.

Common monitoring techniques include vibration analysis for rotating equipment, thermal imaging for electrical systems, and oil analysis for mechanical systems.

This approach is most cost-effective for critical, expensive assets where the cost of monitoring is justified by the savings from avoiding unplanned failures. Large HVAC chillers, generators, and lift systems are common candidates.

Condition-Based Maintenance

Similar to predictive maintenance, condition-based maintenance triggers action when monitored parameters (vibration, temperature, pressure, humidity) reach predefined thresholds. It bridges the gap between fixed-schedule preventive maintenance and fully predictive approaches.

With the increasing availability of affordable IoT sensors, condition-based maintenance is becoming accessible to a wider range of facilities and asset types.

Building a Facility Maintenance Plan

A maintenance plan is the document that defines what needs to be maintained, how often, by whom, and to what standard. Without a plan, maintenance is ad hoc, inconsistent, and impossible to manage effectively.

Step 1: Create an Asset Inventory

List every asset and system in your facility that requires maintenance. Include the asset type, location, manufacturer, model, serial number, installation date, warranty status, and criticality rating.

  • Walk every area of the facility to identify all maintainable assets
  • Group assets by system (HVAC, electrical, plumbing, fire, security, etc.)
  • Rate each asset by criticality to building operation and occupant safety
  • Record manufacturer maintenance recommendations for each asset
  • Note the current condition and any known issues
  • Photograph assets and record their exact locations

This inventory becomes the foundation of your maintenance programme. It is worth investing time to get it right, as every other step depends on having an accurate and complete asset register.

Step 2: Define Maintenance Tasks

For each asset, define the maintenance tasks required and their frequency. Use manufacturer guidelines, industry standards (such as CIBSE, SFG20, or ASHRAE), and your own experience to determine the right intervals.

Document each task with clear instructions that anyone with the appropriate skills could follow. Include required tools and parts, safety precautions, expected completion time, and acceptance criteria.

Be specific. "Check HVAC system" is not a useful task description. "Inspect and clean AHU-01 filters, check belt tension, inspect condensate drain, and record supply air temperature" is actionable and verifiable.

Step 3: Set Priorities

Not all maintenance is equally urgent. Prioritise based on the impact of failure on safety, operations, compliance, and cost.

  • Critical: failure creates immediate safety risk or shuts down the building
  • High: failure significantly disrupts operations or occupant comfort
  • Medium: failure causes inconvenience but operations can continue
  • Low: failure has minimal impact on operations or comfort

Priority determines how quickly you respond to breakdowns and how rigorously you enforce preventive maintenance schedules. Critical and high-priority assets should never have their maintenance deferred.

Step 4: Assign Responsibilities

Decide which tasks are handled by in-house staff and which require specialist contractors. This decision depends on the complexity of the task, the skills of your team, and any regulatory requirements for certified specialists.

Ensure every task has a clear owner. Unassigned tasks do not get completed.

For contractor-managed tasks, establish service level agreements (SLAs) that define response times, service standards, reporting requirements, and performance penalties.

Step 5: Create a Maintenance Calendar

Map all scheduled maintenance tasks onto a calendar. Distribute work evenly to avoid overloading your team during certain periods. Account for seasonal requirements, building usage patterns, and operational constraints.

A visual calendar helps identify conflicts, gaps, and resource bottlenecks before they become problems. It also provides a clear forward view of upcoming work for budgeting and resource planning.

Consider building occupancy when scheduling disruptive maintenance. HVAC work, painting, and noisy repairs are best scheduled outside core business hours where possible.

Step 6: Establish KPIs

Define the metrics you will use to measure maintenance performance. Track these consistently and review them at least monthly.

  • Preventive maintenance completion rate (target: 90%+)
  • Work order response and completion times
  • Reactive vs preventive maintenance ratio
  • Maintenance cost per square metre or per building
  • Equipment uptime and availability for critical systems
  • Compliance audit pass rates and outstanding non-conformances
  • Occupant satisfaction scores and complaint volumes
  • Energy consumption trends (as an indicator of equipment health)

KPIs only work if they are reviewed and acted upon. Data without action is just overhead.

Multi-Site Facility Management

Organisations that manage multiple facilities face additional complexity. Consistency, visibility, and coordination become critical when maintenance spans several locations, each with its own building systems, staff, and contractors.

Standardise Processes

Create standard maintenance procedures, checklists, and workflows that apply across all sites. This ensures consistent quality regardless of which team or contractor performs the work.

Standardisation also makes it easier to compare performance between sites, identify best practices that can be shared, and onboard new sites or staff efficiently.

Standard processes do not mean ignoring site-specific differences. Each building will have unique assets and requirements. The framework should be consistent, with flexibility for local variation where needed.

Centralise Data

Use a single system to manage maintenance across all locations. This gives management visibility into performance at every site without relying on individual site reports that may arrive late, use different formats, or contain inconsistent data.

  • View all open work orders across every facility from one dashboard
  • Compare maintenance costs and performance between sites objectively
  • Identify common issues affecting multiple locations
  • Manage contractors and service providers centrally
  • Consolidate purchasing for spare parts and materials
  • Produce portfolio-wide compliance and performance reports

Empower Local Teams

While processes should be standardised, local teams need the autonomy to manage day-to-day maintenance at their site. The right balance between central control and local flexibility is key to maintaining both consistency and responsiveness.

Give site managers the tools and authority to handle routine issues, create and manage work orders, and make decisions about minor repairs. Escalation paths should be clear for significant issues, budget decisions, and capital projects.

Share Knowledge Across Sites

When one site solves a recurring maintenance problem, that solution should be shared across the portfolio. A centralised CMMS makes this easier by providing visibility into maintenance activities and outcomes at every location.

Regular cross-site reviews, shared procedure libraries, and common training programmes all help spread best practices across a multi-site operation.

Compliance and Safety

Facility maintenance has a direct impact on safety and regulatory compliance. Failure to maintain building systems properly can result in accidents, fines, legal liability, and even criminal prosecution in serious cases.

Statutory Compliance

Most facilities are subject to regulations that require specific maintenance activities to be performed at defined intervals. These vary by jurisdiction and building type but commonly include:

  • Fire safety systems testing, inspection, and servicing
  • Electrical installation testing and certification (EICR)
  • Lift and escalator inspections (LOLER)
  • Legionella risk assessments and water treatment monitoring
  • Gas safety checks and boiler servicing
  • Asbestos management surveys and monitoring
  • Emergency lighting testing (monthly function, annual duration)
  • PAT testing for portable electrical appliances
  • Pressure systems examination (PSSR)

Missing statutory maintenance deadlines is not just bad practice. It is a legal obligation, and non-compliance can have serious consequences including enforcement notices, fines, and building closure orders.

Documentation and Audit Trails

Compliance requires evidence. Every statutory maintenance task must be documented with the date performed, who performed it, their qualifications, the results, and any follow-up actions required.

A CMMS creates this documentation automatically as work is completed. When an auditor or inspector requests records, you can produce them immediately rather than searching through filing cabinets, email inboxes, and contractor files.

The quality of your records reflects the quality of your maintenance operation. Complete, accurate, and easily accessible records demonstrate professionalism and due diligence.

Risk Assessments

Regular risk assessments should be integrated into your maintenance plan. Identify hazards associated with building systems and maintenance activities. Assess the likelihood and severity of harm. Implement controls through maintenance procedures, safety equipment, and training.

Review risk assessments whenever there is a significant change to the facility, equipment, or operations. At minimum, conduct an annual review of all maintenance-related risk assessments.

Permit to Work Systems

High-risk maintenance activities such as hot works, confined space entry, work at height, and electrical isolation require formal permit to work systems. These ensure that hazards are identified, controls are in place, and work is properly authorised before it begins.

A CMMS can manage permit to work workflows, ensuring that permits are issued, controlled, and closed out systematically.

Best Practices for Facility Maintenance

These best practices are drawn from well-managed facility operations across a range of building types and sizes.

Prioritise Preventive Over Reactive

Aim for at least 70-80% of maintenance work to be planned and preventive. Track your reactive vs preventive ratio and work to improve it over time.

Every reactive task should prompt the question: could this have been prevented with a scheduled inspection or service? If the answer is yes, add or adjust the preventive schedule.

Use a CMMS to Manage Maintenance

Spreadsheets and paper-based systems cannot provide the visibility, automation, and reporting that facility maintenance requires at scale. A CMMS is the right tool for the job.

  • Automate preventive maintenance scheduling with automatic reminders
  • Track all work orders from request through to completion and sign-off
  • Maintain a complete asset register with full maintenance history
  • Generate compliance reports and certificates on demand
  • Give technicians mobile access to work orders, procedures, and asset data
  • Track contractor performance against agreed SLAs

Invest in Training

Well-trained maintenance staff work more efficiently, make fewer mistakes, and identify potential problems before they become failures. Invest in both technical skills training and system training for your CMMS and other tools.

Cross-training team members on multiple building systems reduces your dependency on any single individual and improves team resilience. When one person is absent, another can cover their responsibilities.

Build Relationships with Contractors

For specialist maintenance such as lifts, HVAC, fire systems, and electrical testing, having trusted contractor relationships is essential. Good contractors become an extension of your team.

Establish clear expectations, agree on response times, require detailed job reports, and review contractor performance regularly. A CMMS can help manage contractor work orders, track response times, and document completed work for accountability.

Plan for the Long Term

Facility maintenance is not just about today's tasks. Develop a long-term capital plan that accounts for major equipment replacements, building renovations, and system upgrades.

  • Track asset age and condition to forecast replacement needs
  • Budget for major maintenance projects 3-5 years ahead
  • Use maintenance data to support capital investment decisions
  • Consider lifecycle cost rather than just purchase price when buying new equipment
  • Plan for energy efficiency improvements alongside maintenance

Communicate with Building Occupants

Good communication reduces complaints and helps occupants understand the purpose of maintenance activities. Notify people in advance of planned work that may cause noise, disruption, or temporary loss of services.

Provide a simple way for occupants to report issues. A well-managed request system ensures problems are captured, acknowledged, and addressed promptly. Quick response to reported issues builds trust and encourages further reporting.

Technology for Facility Maintenance

Technology plays an increasingly important role in facility maintenance. The right tools improve efficiency, provide better data, and enable more proactive management.

CMMS and Facility Management Software

A CMMS is the core technology for facility maintenance. It manages assets, schedules maintenance, tracks work orders, and provides reporting. Cloud-based CMMS platforms offer the advantage of access from anywhere, automatic updates, and no on-premise servers to manage.

For facility teams, look for a CMMS that handles both reactive and preventive work orders, supports contractor management, and provides compliance tracking and reporting.

Mobile Applications

Mobile access allows technicians to receive work orders, update task status, record findings, and capture photos directly from the field. This eliminates paperwork and ensures data is captured in real time rather than being written up at the end of the day (or forgotten entirely).

  • Receive and update work orders on the move
  • Access equipment manuals and maintenance procedures on site
  • Log meter readings and condition observations during inspections
  • Scan QR codes on assets to pull up records instantly
  • Capture photos of defects, completed work, and readings
  • Record time spent on each task automatically

IoT and Condition Monitoring

Internet of Things sensors can monitor building systems continuously, detecting issues before they become failures. Temperature, humidity, vibration, energy consumption, water flow, and air quality data can all be captured automatically.

When integrated with a CMMS, sensor data can trigger work orders automatically when parameters exceed normal ranges. This moves maintenance from a scheduled approach to a condition-based approach for monitored systems.

Building Management Systems (BMS)

A BMS controls and monitors building services like HVAC, lighting, and access control. Integrating your BMS with your CMMS connects operational data with maintenance management for a more complete picture of building performance.

BMS alarms can feed into the CMMS as work order requests, ensuring that system faults are captured and tracked through to resolution.

Energy Management

Monitoring energy consumption alongside maintenance data helps identify equipment that is underperforming or operating inefficiently. Poorly maintained equipment often consumes more energy. A chiller with dirty coils, an AHU with blocked filters, or a boiler running below optimal efficiency all waste energy.

Linking energy data to maintenance activities demonstrates the direct connection between good maintenance and lower operating costs.

Common Facility Maintenance Mistakes

Even with a plan in place, facility maintenance teams can fall into patterns that reduce effectiveness. Being aware of these common mistakes helps you avoid them.

Deferring Maintenance to Save Money

When budgets are tight, maintenance is often one of the first areas to be cut. This creates a backlog of deferred maintenance that grows more expensive over time. Small problems that could have been fixed cheaply become major failures that require significant capital expenditure.

Deferred maintenance is a false economy. The money saved in the short term is always exceeded by the cost of the failures and emergency repairs that follow.

Ignoring Manufacturer Recommendations

Equipment manufacturers publish maintenance schedules for a reason. They know their equipment better than anyone. Ignoring or significantly reducing recommended maintenance frequencies increases the risk of failure and can void warranties.

Use manufacturer recommendations as a baseline and adjust based on your operating conditions and experience. Do not simply skip maintenance tasks to save time.

Relying on One Person

When all maintenance knowledge resides with a single individual, the organisation is vulnerable. That person's absence due to illness, holiday, or departure creates an immediate gap in maintenance capability.

  • Document procedures so others can perform critical tasks
  • Cross-train team members on essential systems
  • Use a CMMS to capture institutional knowledge digitally
  • Ensure contractor contact details and procedures are accessible to multiple people

Not Tracking Maintenance Costs

Without visibility into what maintenance is costing per asset, per building, or per system, it is impossible to make informed decisions about repairs, replacements, or budget allocation. A CMMS that tracks labour and parts costs against each work order provides this visibility automatically.

Treating All Assets the Same

Not every asset deserves the same level of maintenance attention. Critical systems that affect safety, compliance, or core operations should receive more frequent and rigorous maintenance than non-critical assets. A risk-based approach ensures resources are focused where they have the greatest impact.

Getting Started

If your facility maintenance is currently reactive or paper-based, you do not need to overhaul everything at once. Start with these practical steps and build from there.

  • List your critical assets and their current maintenance status
  • Identify your statutory compliance requirements and check you are meeting them
  • Set up preventive maintenance schedules for your most critical systems first
  • Implement a CMMS to manage and track maintenance digitally
  • Train your team on the new processes and tools
  • Establish a simple reporting routine to review performance monthly
  • Expand your programme over time to cover more assets and systems

Consistent improvement over time is more sustainable than trying to achieve perfection immediately. Focus on building good habits, capturing reliable data, and using that data to drive decisions.

Summary

Effective facility maintenance requires a structured approach: a clear plan, the right tools, trained people, and consistent execution. The best practices in this guide apply whether you manage a single building or a portfolio of sites across multiple locations.

The shift from reactive to preventive maintenance is the single most impactful change most facility teams can make. It reduces costs, improves safety, ensures compliance, and makes maintenance more predictable and manageable.

A CMMS provides the structure and automation to make that shift sustainable. Combined with good processes, skilled people, and management commitment, it transforms facility maintenance from a cost centre into a strategic function that protects asset value and supports business operations.
