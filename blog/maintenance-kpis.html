You cannot improve what you do not measure. That is especially true in maintenance, where the difference between a well-run operation and a chaotic one often comes down to visibility — knowing what is working, what is not, and where to focus.

Maintenance KPIs (Key Performance Indicators) give you that visibility. They translate day-to-day maintenance activities into numbers you can track, compare, and act on.

This guide covers the KPIs that matter most, what they measure, and how to use them to drive real improvement.

What Are Maintenance KPIs?

Maintenance KPIs are quantifiable metrics that measure the effectiveness of your maintenance operations. They answer fundamental questions: Are we preventing breakdowns? Are we using our resources efficiently? Are we keeping costs under control?

Good maintenance KPIs share these characteristics:

  • Measurable — based on data you can actually collect
  • Actionable — the number tells you something you can act on
  • Relevant — aligned with your operational goals
  • Timely — available when you need to make decisions
  • Comparable — you can benchmark against past performance or industry standards

The goal is not to track everything. It is to track the right things. Too many KPIs create noise. Too few leave blind spots. Most maintenance teams should focus on 8-10 core metrics.

1. Mean Time Between Failures (MTBF)

MTBF measures the average time between equipment failures. It tells you how reliable your assets are and whether reliability is improving or declining over time.

How to calculate:

MTBF = Total operating time / Number of failures

Example: A motor runs for 2,000 hours and fails 4 times. MTBF = 500 hours.

What it tells you:

  • Rising MTBF means your assets are becoming more reliable
  • Falling MTBF signals increasing problems — investigate root causes
  • Compare MTBF across similar assets to identify underperformers
  • Use MTBF to set preventive maintenance intervals

MTBF is one of the most widely used reliability metrics. A steady increase in MTBF is one of the clearest signs that your maintenance program is working.

2. Mean Time to Repair (MTTR)

MTTR measures the average time it takes to repair an asset after a failure. It covers the full repair cycle: diagnosis, parts procurement, repair work, and testing.

How to calculate:

MTTR = Total repair time / Number of repairs

Example: Four repairs take a total of 12 hours. MTTR = 3 hours per repair.

What it tells you:

  • High MTTR suggests skill gaps, parts availability issues, or poor diagnostics
  • Tracking MTTR by technician can identify training needs
  • Tracking MTTR by asset type reveals which equipment is hardest to maintain
  • Reducing MTTR directly reduces downtime

The fastest way to reduce MTTR is to ensure spare parts are available, maintenance procedures are documented, and technicians have the right training.

3. PM Compliance Rate

PM compliance measures the percentage of scheduled preventive maintenance tasks that are completed on time. It is a direct indicator of how well your PM program is being executed.

How to calculate:

PM Compliance = (Completed PMs on time / Total scheduled PMs) x 100

Example: 90 out of 100 scheduled PMs completed on time = 90% compliance.

What it tells you:

  • Below 80% means your PM program is not being followed — find out why
  • 90%+ is the target for a well-run maintenance operation
  • Low compliance often points to understaffing, poor scheduling, or lack of accountability
  • Falling compliance predicts a future rise in unplanned breakdowns

PM compliance is the leading indicator that everything else depends on. If scheduled maintenance is not getting done, every other metric will suffer.

4. Maintenance Backlog

Maintenance backlog is the total amount of maintenance work that has been identified but not yet completed. It is typically measured in hours or days of work outstanding.

How to calculate:

Backlog = Total hours of approved but incomplete work orders

What it tells you:

  • A healthy backlog is 2-4 weeks of work — enough to keep the team busy without falling behind
  • Growing backlog means work is being created faster than it is completed
  • Very low backlog may mean problems are not being identified or reported
  • Excessive backlog leads to deferred maintenance and increased failure risk

Backlog is a capacity metric. It tells you whether you have the right number of people and resources to keep up with your maintenance workload.

5. Planned vs Unplanned Maintenance Ratio

This ratio compares the amount of planned (preventive) maintenance work to unplanned (reactive) work. It is one of the most revealing metrics about the maturity of your maintenance operation.

How to calculate:

Ratio = Planned work orders / Total work orders x 100

What it tells you:

  • Below 50% planned — you are in firefighting mode. Most work is reactive
  • 50-70% planned — improving but still too much reactive work
  • 70-80% planned — good. Your PM program is working
  • 80%+ planned — excellent. This is the target for a mature maintenance operation
  • 90%+ planned — world-class maintenance performance

This single metric tells you more about your maintenance maturity than almost any other. Track it monthly and aim for steady improvement.

6. Overall Equipment Effectiveness (OEE)

OEE is the gold standard for measuring manufacturing productivity. It combines three factors — availability, performance, and quality — into a single percentage.

How to calculate:

OEE = Availability x Performance x Quality

Where:

  • Availability = Actual run time / Planned production time (accounts for downtime)
  • Performance = Actual output / Maximum possible output (accounts for speed losses)
  • Quality = Good units / Total units produced (accounts for defects)

What it tells you:

  • An OEE of 100% means perfect production — no downtime, full speed, zero defects
  • 85% OEE is considered world-class for manufacturing
  • Most plants operate between 40-60% OEE
  • Maintenance directly impacts the availability component

While OEE is an operations metric rather than a pure maintenance metric, maintenance teams have a direct impact on the availability component. Reducing unplanned downtime is the most impactful thing maintenance can do to improve OEE.

7. Maintenance Cost as Percentage of Asset Replacement Value

This metric measures how much you spend on maintaining an asset relative to what it would cost to replace it. It helps identify assets that are costing more to maintain than they are worth.

How to calculate:

Maintenance Cost % = (Annual maintenance cost / Asset replacement value) x 100

What it tells you:

  • Industry benchmark is 2-5% of replacement value per year
  • Above 5% suggests the asset may need major refurbishment or replacement
  • Consistently rising costs signal an asset nearing end of useful life
  • Helps justify capital expenditure for new equipment

This metric is particularly useful in budget discussions. When you can show that an asset is consuming 8% of its replacement value annually in maintenance, the business case for replacement becomes clear.

8. Work Order Completion Rate

Work order completion rate measures the percentage of work orders that are completed within a given period. It reflects the productivity and capacity of your maintenance team.

How to calculate:

Completion Rate = (Completed work orders / Total work orders issued) x 100

What it tells you:

  • Below 80% suggests capacity issues, poor prioritisation, or inefficient processes
  • 90%+ is the target for a well-functioning maintenance team
  • Declining completion rate indicates growing workload or shrinking resources
  • Break down by priority level to see if urgent work crowds out planned maintenance

A low completion rate combined with a growing backlog is a clear signal that your team needs more resources, better tools, or more efficient processes.

9. Mean Time to Acknowledge (MTTA)

MTTA measures the average time between when a work order is created and when a technician acknowledges and begins working on it. It captures the response time of your maintenance team.

How to calculate:

MTTA = Total time from work order creation to acknowledgement / Number of work orders

What it tells you:

  • High MTTA means work orders are sitting in a queue too long
  • Often caused by poor notification systems or unclear assignment
  • A CMMS with mobile notifications can reduce MTTA significantly
  • Track separately for emergency vs planned work orders

MTTA is the gap between "we know there is a problem" and "someone is working on it." Reducing this gap reduces total downtime.

10. Inventory Turnover Rate

Inventory turnover measures how quickly your spare parts inventory is being used and replenished. It helps you optimise stock levels — carrying enough parts to avoid delays without tying up capital in excess inventory.

How to calculate:

Inventory Turnover = Cost of parts used / Average inventory value

What it tells you:

  • Low turnover means you are overstocked — capital is tied up in parts sitting on shelves
  • Very high turnover may mean you are understocked — risking stockouts and delays
  • Identify slow-moving parts that may be obsolete
  • Identify frequently used parts that need higher reorder points

A well-managed spare parts inventory reduces MTTR by ensuring parts are available when needed, while minimising the cost of holding excess stock.

How to Track Maintenance KPIs

Knowing which KPIs to track is the first step. Actually collecting and reporting on them consistently is where most teams struggle.

Manual Tracking

Spreadsheets and paper forms can work for small operations with few assets. The limitations become obvious quickly: data entry is slow, errors are common, and generating reports takes hours. Trends are hard to spot when data lives in disconnected files.

CMMS-Based Tracking

A CMMS collects KPI data automatically as part of normal maintenance operations. When a technician completes a work order, the system records the time, cost, and parts used. Reports and dashboards are generated in real time.

A CMMS gives you:

  • Automatic data collection — KPIs are calculated from work order data, no manual entry needed
  • Real-time dashboards — see current performance at a glance
  • Trend analysis — compare performance across weeks, months, or years
  • Drill-down capability — click on a metric to see the underlying data
  • Alerts and notifications — get notified when a KPI crosses a threshold
  • Exportable reports — share data with management and stakeholders

Best Practices for KPI Tracking

  • Start with 5-6 KPIs and add more as your data quality improves
  • Review KPIs weekly with your maintenance team
  • Present monthly KPI reports to management
  • Set specific targets for each KPI based on your current baseline
  • Focus on trends rather than individual data points
  • Use KPIs to drive action, not just to generate reports

How a CMMS Helps

The biggest barrier to effective KPI tracking is data. Without a system that captures maintenance data consistently, your KPIs are based on estimates and guesswork. A CMMS removes this barrier.

Here is how a CMMS supports each KPI:

  • MTBF and MTTR — automatically calculated from work order timestamps and asset failure records
  • PM Compliance — the system knows what was scheduled and what was completed on time
  • Backlog — real-time count of open work orders with estimated hours
  • Planned vs Unplanned — work orders are categorised automatically based on type
  • OEE — availability data feeds directly from downtime tracking
  • Cost tracking — labour hours, parts costs, and contractor costs recorded per asset
  • Work order completion — tracked automatically as work orders move through their lifecycle
  • Inventory — parts usage, stock levels, and reorder points managed in one system

Without a CMMS, tracking KPIs is a manual, time-consuming exercise that most teams eventually abandon. With a CMMS, it happens automatically as part of daily work.

Getting Started

Do not try to track everything on day one. Start with the metrics that matter most to your operation and build from there.

A practical starting point for most teams:

  • PM Compliance Rate — are you doing the preventive work you planned?
  • Planned vs Unplanned Ratio — how much of your work is proactive?
  • Work Order Completion Rate — is your team keeping up with the workload?
  • MTTR — how quickly are you resolving breakdowns?
  • Maintenance Backlog — is your outstanding work growing or shrinking?

Once these five metrics are being tracked consistently and driving decisions, add MTBF, OEE, and cost-based metrics. The key is to use the data, not just collect it. Every KPI should lead to a question, and every question should lead to an action.

The maintenance teams that measure their performance are the ones that improve it. Start measuring today.
