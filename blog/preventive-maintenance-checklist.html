Introduction

A preventive maintenance (PM) checklist ensures equipment is inspected, serviced and maintained on schedule — before failures, not after. It's one of the most effective tools in any reliability programme, because it turns "we usually remember to do this" into a repeatable, auditable process.

Using a structured checklist improves consistency across shifts, reduces downtime, creates an audit trail for compliance, and extends asset lifespan.

This guide covers what a PM checklist is, what to include at daily, weekly, monthly and annual intervals, how to build one for your own equipment, and the mistakes to avoid. For the broader context — including inspection, safety and pre-start checklists — see our complete maintenance checklists guide.

What is a Preventive Maintenance Checklist?

A preventive maintenance checklist is a list of tasks performed at scheduled intervals to maintain equipment performance.

It defines:

  • What needs to be checked or serviced
  • How often each task should be performed
  • Who is responsible for completing the work
  • What to record after each inspection

A well-structured checklist ensures that nothing is missed and maintenance is performed consistently.

Why Use a Checklist?

Without a checklist, preventive maintenance becomes inconsistent and unreliable.

A structured checklist:

  • Ensures consistency across technicians and shifts
  • Reduces human error and missed tasks
  • Improves efficiency by providing clear instructions
  • Standardises maintenance processes across your organisation
  • Creates a record for compliance and auditing

Example Preventive Maintenance Checklist

Below is a general-purpose checklist structure. Adapt it to suit your specific equipment and operations.

Daily Checks

Quick inspections performed at the start or end of each shift.

  • Visual inspection of equipment for obvious damage or wear
  • Check for leaks (oil, water, hydraulic fluid)
  • Monitor performance indicators and gauges
  • Check safety guards and emergency stops
  • Report any unusual noises or vibrations

Weekly Checks

More thorough inspections performed once per week.

  • Clean equipment and surrounding areas
  • Inspect belts, hoses, and connections
  • Check fluid levels and top up as needed
  • Test safety systems and alarms
  • Inspect filters and clean or replace if required

Monthly Checks

Detailed servicing tasks performed on a monthly cycle.

  • Lubricate moving parts and bearings
  • Inspect and replace worn components
  • Test equipment performance against specifications
  • Check electrical connections and wiring
  • Review maintenance logs for recurring issues

Annual Maintenance

Comprehensive servicing performed once per year.

  • Full equipment inspection and condition assessment
  • Major component servicing or replacement
  • Calibration of instruments and sensors
  • Review and update maintenance schedules
  • Update asset records with condition data

How to Implement a PM Checklist

Follow these steps to get started:

Step 1: Identify Critical Assets

Start with the equipment that has the biggest impact on operations if it fails.

Step 2: Define Maintenance Tasks

For each asset, list the specific tasks required at each interval (daily, weekly, monthly, annual).

Step 3: Set Frequencies

Determine how often each task should be performed based on manufacturer recommendations, usage, and history.

Step 4: Assign Responsibilities

Allocate tasks to specific technicians or teams.

Step 5: Use Software to Automate

Use a preventive maintenance system to automate scheduling and track completion — replacing paper and spreadsheets.

Step 6: Track and Review

Monitor completion rates and review performance regularly to identify improvements.

Best Practices

  • Standardise procedures — use the same checklist format across all assets
  • Train technicians — ensure everyone understands what is expected
  • Track completion — use a CMMS to record all activity
  • Use mobile tools — allow technicians to complete checklists on the shop floor
  • Review regularly — update checklists based on findings and equipment changes
  • Start simple — begin with critical assets and expand over time

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Creating checklists that are too long or complex
  • Not tracking whether tasks are actually completed
  • Using paper checklists that get lost or ignored
  • Failing to update checklists as equipment changes
  • Not involving technicians in the checklist design

Conclusion

A preventive maintenance checklist is a simple but powerful tool for improving reliability and reducing downtime.

By standardising tasks, automating scheduling, and tracking completion, organisations can shift from reactive to proactive maintenance — improving efficiency and extending the life of their assets.
