Predictive maintenance (PdM) is a maintenance strategy that uses real-time data and condition-monitoring techniques to predict when equipment is likely to fail. Instead of waiting for a breakdown or servicing on a fixed schedule, maintenance is performed only when data indicates it is actually needed.

The goal is straightforward: fix things before they break, without wasting effort on equipment that is still running fine. This guide explains how predictive maintenance works, the technologies behind it, and how it compares to other approaches.

How Predictive Maintenance Works

Predictive maintenance follows a continuous loop of monitoring, analysis, and action. At its core, sensors collect data from equipment during normal operation. That data is then analysed to detect patterns or anomalies that signal an emerging problem.

The typical process looks like this:

  • Sensors are installed on critical equipment to measure vibration, temperature, pressure, or other parameters
  • Data is collected continuously or at regular intervals and transmitted to a central system
  • Analysis tools compare current readings against known baselines and historical trends
  • When readings exceed defined thresholds or show abnormal patterns, an alert is raised
  • A maintenance team investigates and schedules a repair before failure occurs

The key difference from other strategies is that the trigger for maintenance is actual equipment condition — not a calendar date and not a breakdown.

Over time, the system accumulates data that makes predictions more accurate. The longer you monitor an asset, the better the system understands what normal behaviour looks like and what early warning signs precede a failure.

Predictive vs Preventive vs Reactive Maintenance

Understanding predictive maintenance is easier when you see how it stacks up against the other common approaches. Each strategy has a different trigger, cost profile, and level of downtime risk.

Aspect Reactive Preventive Predictive
When maintenance happens After failure On a fixed schedule When data shows it is needed
Downtime risk High Medium Low
Cost profile Low upfront, high repair costs Moderate and predictable Higher upfront, lowest overall
Data required None Manufacturer recommendations Real-time sensor data
Equipment life impact Shortened Average Extended
Labour efficiency Poor — emergency response Moderate — some unnecessary tasks High — targeted interventions
Spare parts planning Reactive — emergency orders Scheduled — bulk purchasing Targeted — order what you need, when you need it
Best suited for Non-critical, low-cost assets Standard equipment with known wear patterns Critical, high-value, or complex assets

In practice, most organisations use a combination of all three strategies. Predictive maintenance is applied to the most critical and expensive assets, where unplanned downtime has the greatest impact. Preventive maintenance covers standard equipment with predictable wear. Reactive maintenance is reserved for low-cost, non-critical items where the cost of prevention exceeds the cost of replacement.

The shift towards predictive maintenance does not mean abandoning the other approaches. It means being more strategic about where you invest monitoring resources for the greatest return.

Technologies Used in Predictive Maintenance

Predictive maintenance relies on several monitoring technologies. The right choice depends on the equipment type, failure modes, and operating environment. Most programmes use a combination of techniques to cover the full range of potential failure modes.

Vibration Analysis

Vibration sensors detect changes in the vibration signature of rotating equipment such as motors, pumps, and compressors. An increase in vibration often indicates bearing wear, imbalance, misalignment, or looseness.

This is one of the most widely used predictive techniques because it covers a broad range of failure modes on common industrial equipment. Modern wireless accelerometers can be installed quickly and transmit data continuously without the need for manual collection rounds.

Temperature Monitoring

Abnormal temperature changes can signal friction, electrical faults, or fluid issues. Temperature sensors and thermal imaging cameras are used to identify hot spots on electrical panels, bearings, and other components before they fail.

Continuous temperature monitoring is particularly valuable for electrical distribution systems, where loose connections generate heat long before they cause a fire or outage. In mechanical systems, rising temperatures often indicate lubrication failure or excessive friction.

Oil and Fluid Analysis

Analysing lubricants and hydraulic fluids reveals contamination, wear particles, and chemical changes. This is particularly useful for gearboxes, engines, and hydraulic systems where fluid condition directly affects performance and longevity.

Oil analysis can detect problems that other techniques miss — for example, internal wear that generates metal particles too small to affect vibration readings. Regular sampling provides a trend that shows how quickly fluid is degrading.

Ultrasonic Testing

Ultrasonic sensors detect high-frequency sounds that are beyond human hearing. These sounds can indicate leaks in compressed air or gas systems, electrical discharge (arcing), or early-stage bearing defects.

Compressed air leaks alone can account for 20-30% of a compressor's output in poorly maintained facilities. Ultrasonic detection identifies leaks quickly and accurately, leading to immediate energy savings.

Power and Current Monitoring

Monitoring the electrical current drawn by motors and other equipment can reveal mechanical problems. A motor drawing more current than normal may be experiencing increased load due to a mechanical fault such as a worn belt or failing bearing.

Current monitoring is non-invasive — clamp-on sensors can be installed without shutting down the equipment. This makes it a practical technique for motors that run continuously and cannot be easily taken offline for inspection.

Flow and Pressure Monitoring

Sensors that measure flow rate or pressure in piping and hydraulic systems can detect blockages, leaks, or pump degradation. Deviations from normal operating parameters trigger further investigation.

For example, a gradual decline in pump output pressure at constant speed is a reliable indicator of impeller wear. Catching this trend early allows you to schedule a rebuild during a planned shutdown rather than dealing with an unexpected failure during production.

Benefits of Predictive Maintenance

Organisations that implement predictive maintenance effectively can expect measurable improvements across several areas.

  • Reduced unplanned downtime — failures are anticipated and prevented before they stop production
  • Lower maintenance costs — work is done only when needed, eliminating unnecessary scheduled tasks
  • Extended equipment life — early intervention prevents secondary damage and reduces wear
  • Better spare parts management — knowing what will fail and when allows targeted inventory planning
  • Improved safety — catching problems early reduces the chance of catastrophic failures
  • Higher labour efficiency — technicians focus on targeted repairs instead of routine inspections
  • Data-driven decisions — historical trends help optimise asset replacement and capital planning
  • Reduced energy consumption — equipment running in poor condition often consumes more energy

Studies consistently show that predictive maintenance programmes can reduce maintenance costs by 25-30% and cut unplanned downtime by up to 70% compared to reactive approaches.

The financial case is compelling: even a single prevented failure on a critical production asset can pay for an entire year of monitoring across multiple machines.

The Role of IoT in Predictive Maintenance

The Internet of Things (IoT) has made predictive maintenance far more accessible. Traditionally, condition monitoring required expensive specialist equipment and manual data collection rounds. A trained analyst would visit each machine with a handheld device, take readings, and upload the data for review. This was effective but expensive, slow, and limited in scope.

IoT changes this by providing affordable, always-on sensors that stream data automatically. The benefits are significant:

  • Continuous data collection without manual intervention
  • Remote monitoring of equipment across multiple sites
  • Automatic alerts when readings exceed thresholds
  • Integration with CMMS to automatically generate work orders
  • Lower cost per monitoring point compared to traditional systems
  • Faster detection of rapidly developing faults that would be missed between manual rounds

When IoT sensors are connected to a CMMS platform, the entire predictive maintenance workflow — from detection to work order to completion — can be managed in one system. This eliminates the gap between knowing about a problem and acting on it.

IoT has also democratised predictive maintenance. Previously, only large organisations with dedicated reliability engineering teams could justify the cost. Now, small and medium-sized businesses can monitor their most critical assets at a fraction of the traditional cost.

Common Challenges

Predictive maintenance delivers strong results, but implementation is not without challenges. Being aware of these upfront helps you plan effectively and set realistic expectations.

  • Initial investment — sensors, connectivity infrastructure, and software require upfront capital
  • Data quality — poor sensor placement or unreliable connectivity can lead to false readings
  • Baseline data — the system needs time to learn what "normal" looks like for each asset
  • Skills gap — maintenance teams may need training to interpret data and act on insights
  • Integration complexity — connecting sensors to existing maintenance systems can be difficult
  • Change management — teams accustomed to fixed schedules need time to trust data-driven decisions
  • Alert fatigue — poorly configured thresholds generate too many false alarms, eroding confidence

Many of these challenges are reduced by choosing a platform that combines IoT hardware and CMMS software in a single solution, avoiding the need to integrate separate systems. Starting with a small number of assets also keeps complexity manageable while the team builds experience and confidence.

Getting Started with Predictive Maintenance

You do not need to monitor every asset from day one. A phased approach works best and delivers value quickly while keeping the project manageable. Here is a practical roadmap for getting started.

Step 1: Identify Critical Assets

Start by listing the assets where unplanned failure has the greatest impact on production, safety, or cost. These are your best candidates for predictive monitoring. Common starting points include pumps, motors, compressors, and HVAC systems.

Use your maintenance history to identify which assets have caused the most downtime or the highest repair costs over the past 12-24 months. This gives you a data-driven starting point rather than guessing.

Step 2: Understand Failure Modes

For each critical asset, identify how it typically fails. A motor might fail due to bearing wear (detectable by vibration), while an electrical panel might overheat (detectable by temperature). The failure mode determines which sensor type you need.

Talk to your technicians — they often know the early warning signs from experience, even if that knowledge has never been formally documented. Their input is invaluable for selecting the right monitoring approach.

Step 3: Install Sensors and Establish Baselines

Deploy sensors on your selected assets and allow the system to collect baseline data over two to four weeks. This baseline represents normal operating conditions and is used as the reference point for detecting anomalies. Make sure the baseline period covers typical operating conditions, not just startup or shutdown periods.

Step 4: Set Thresholds and Alerts

Define the conditions that should trigger an alert. These may be based on absolute values (e.g., temperature above 80 degrees Celsius) or rate-of-change patterns (e.g., vibration increasing by 20% over two weeks). Start with conservative thresholds and refine them as you accumulate data and experience.

Step 5: Connect to Your CMMS

Integrate sensor alerts with your maintenance management system so that work orders are created automatically when thresholds are exceeded. This closes the loop between detection and action. Without this step, alerts are just notifications that rely on someone remembering to act on them.

Step 6: Review and Expand

After running predictive maintenance on your initial assets for three to six months, review the results. Measure downtime reduction, cost savings, and response times. Use these insights to refine your approach and gradually extend monitoring to additional assets.

Document your findings and share them with the wider team. Demonstrating tangible results builds support for expanding the programme and helps secure budget for additional sensors and training.

Who Benefits Most from Predictive Maintenance?

Predictive maintenance delivers the greatest return in environments where:

  • Equipment failures cause significant production losses or safety risks
  • Assets are expensive to repair or replace
  • Downtime costs are high relative to the cost of monitoring
  • Equipment operates continuously or in harsh conditions
  • Compliance or regulatory requirements demand documented maintenance evidence
  • Multiple sites need to be monitored with limited maintenance staff

Industries such as manufacturing, energy, utilities, facilities management, and fleet operations commonly adopt predictive maintenance for these reasons. However, any organisation with critical assets can benefit from a data-driven approach.

Even smaller operations can see significant returns. A single prevented failure on a critical asset — avoiding emergency repairs, production losses, and overtime labour — often justifies the entire monitoring investment.

Real-World Applications

Predictive maintenance is used across a wide range of industries and equipment types. Here are some common applications that demonstrate its versatility.

Manufacturing

Production line motors, conveyors, and CNC machines are monitored for vibration and temperature. Early detection of bearing wear or misalignment prevents unplanned line stoppages that can cost thousands per hour.

Facilities Management

HVAC systems, chillers, and building services equipment are monitored to maintain comfort and efficiency. Temperature and current monitoring on air handling units detects fan bearing degradation and refrigerant issues before they affect tenants.

Energy and Utilities

Turbines, generators, and distribution equipment are monitored for vibration, temperature, and oil condition. The consequences of failure are severe — both financially and in terms of public safety — making predictive maintenance essential for critical infrastructure.

Food and Beverage

Refrigeration systems, packaging machines, and processing equipment are monitored to prevent failures that could cause product spoilage, contamination, or production delays. Temperature monitoring is especially critical for cold chain compliance.

Predictive Maintenance and CMMS

A CMMS is the operational backbone that turns predictive data into action. Without it, sensor data exists in isolation — alerts may be raised but there is no structured process to ensure the right technician responds with the right parts at the right time.

A well-integrated CMMS enables you to:

  • Automatically create work orders from sensor alerts
  • Assign tasks to the right technician based on skills and availability
  • Track the full history of condition data alongside maintenance records
  • Report on the effectiveness of predictive interventions over time
  • Manage preventive schedules alongside predictive triggers in one system
  • Provide mobile access so technicians can view sensor data and asset history on site

This integration is what separates organisations that simply collect data from those that use it to genuinely improve maintenance outcomes. The CMMS ensures that every alert leads to a tracked, accountable action.

Measuring Success

To justify and sustain a predictive maintenance programme, you need to measure its impact. Track these key metrics from the start:

  • Unplanned downtime — compare before and after implementing predictive monitoring
  • Mean time between failures (MTBF) — this should increase as you catch problems earlier
  • Maintenance cost per asset — should decrease as unnecessary preventive tasks are reduced
  • Alert accuracy — track the ratio of true alerts to false alarms and refine thresholds accordingly
  • Response time — measure how quickly the team acts on alerts from detection to resolution
  • Prevented failures — document each instance where monitoring caught a problem before failure

Regular reporting on these metrics demonstrates the value of the programme to management and helps identify areas for improvement. It also provides the evidence needed to expand monitoring to additional assets.

Summary

Predictive maintenance is a data-driven strategy that uses real-time monitoring to detect problems before they cause failures. It reduces downtime, lowers costs, and extends equipment life by ensuring maintenance happens at exactly the right time — not too early, not too late.

The combination of IoT sensors and CMMS software has made predictive maintenance more practical and affordable than ever. By starting with your most critical assets and expanding gradually, any organisation can begin capturing the benefits of a predictive approach.

The key to success is not the technology alone — it is connecting monitoring data to a structured maintenance workflow that ensures every insight leads to action.
