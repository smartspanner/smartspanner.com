Choosing the right maintenance strategy is one of the most impactful decisions a maintenance team can make. Two of the most common approaches are time-based maintenance (TBM) and condition-based maintenance (CBM). Both aim to prevent unexpected failures, but they work in fundamentally different ways.

This article explains how each strategy works, compares them side by side, and helps you decide when to use one, the other, or both together.

What is Time-Based Maintenance?

Time-based maintenance (TBM) is the traditional form of preventive maintenance. Tasks are scheduled at fixed intervals — every 30 days, every 500 operating hours, or every quarter — regardless of the current condition of the equipment.

The schedule is typically based on:

  • Manufacturer recommendations for service intervals
  • Industry standards and regulatory requirements
  • Historical experience with how often equipment fails
  • Calendar-based intervals (weekly, monthly, annually)
  • Usage-based intervals (operating hours, production cycles, mileage)

TBM is straightforward to implement and manage. You define a schedule, assign tasks, and your team executes them on time. A maintenance scheduling system automates the generation and tracking of these recurring tasks.

This approach has been the backbone of preventive maintenance programmes for decades, and it remains effective for many types of equipment and maintenance activities.

Examples of Time-Based Maintenance

  • Changing oil in a gearbox every 3,000 operating hours
  • Replacing air filters every 90 days
  • Inspecting fire safety equipment annually
  • Lubricating bearings every two weeks
  • Testing emergency backup generators monthly
  • Calibrating instruments every six months
  • Servicing HVAC systems at the start of each season

What is Condition-Based Maintenance?

Condition-based maintenance (CBM) schedules maintenance only when monitoring data indicates that equipment condition has deteriorated to a point where intervention is needed. Instead of maintaining on a fixed calendar, maintenance is triggered by actual evidence of wear, degradation, or abnormality.

The fundamental principle behind CBM is that most equipment does not fail on a predictable schedule. Wear rates vary depending on load, operating conditions, environment, and dozens of other factors. By measuring actual condition, you intervene at exactly the right time.

CBM relies on:

  • Sensors that continuously or periodically measure equipment parameters
  • Defined thresholds that indicate when action is required
  • Analysis of trends to detect gradual degradation before it reaches critical levels
  • Integration with a CMMS to turn alerts into work orders automatically

The parameters monitored depend on the equipment type and failure mode. Common measurements include vibration, temperature, pressure, flow rate, electrical current, and oil condition.

Examples of Condition-Based Maintenance

  • Replacing a bearing when vibration levels exceed a defined threshold
  • Servicing a compressor when discharge pressure drops below acceptable levels
  • Changing oil when analysis shows contamination or degradation beyond limits
  • Cleaning a heat exchanger when temperature differential indicates fouling
  • Replacing a filter when differential pressure reaches a set limit
  • Investigating a motor when current draw increases beyond normal range
  • Tightening electrical connections when thermal imaging shows hot spots

Comparison: Condition-Based vs Time-Based Maintenance

The table below provides a direct comparison across the factors that matter most when choosing a maintenance strategy.

Factor Time-Based (TBM) Condition-Based (CBM)
Maintenance trigger Fixed schedule or interval Actual equipment condition
Implementation cost Low — no sensors or monitoring systems needed Higher — requires sensors, connectivity, and software
Ongoing cost May include unnecessary maintenance tasks Maintenance only when needed, reducing waste
Downtime risk Moderate — failures can still occur between intervals Lower — problems detected as they develop
Over-maintenance risk High — parts replaced regardless of condition Low — intervention only when data justifies it
Under-maintenance risk Possible if intervals are set too long Low — continuous monitoring catches degradation early
Data requirements Minimal — just a schedule and task list Continuous sensor data and threshold analysis

Neither strategy is universally better. The right choice depends on the specific asset, its criticality, its failure behaviour, and the consequences of downtime.

Pros and Cons of Time-Based Maintenance

Advantages

  • Simple to plan, schedule, and execute with standard tools
  • Low implementation cost — no sensors or monitoring technology needed
  • Predictable workload and resource requirements for planning purposes
  • Easy to demonstrate compliance with regulatory or warranty requirements
  • Works well for assets with well-understood, consistent wear patterns
  • Familiar to maintenance teams — requires minimal training to adopt
  • Provides a structured foundation for teams transitioning from reactive maintenance

Disadvantages

  • Leads to over-maintenance — replacing parts that still have useful life remaining
  • Does not account for varying operating conditions or load patterns
  • Failures can still occur between scheduled intervals if degradation is faster than expected
  • Wastes labour, parts, and consumables on equipment that does not need attention
  • Maintenance frequency is a compromise — too often wastes resources, too infrequent risks failure
  • No visibility into actual equipment health between scheduled tasks

Pros and Cons of Condition-Based Maintenance

Advantages

  • Maintenance happens only when data justifies it, reducing waste and unnecessary interventions
  • Catches problems that develop between fixed schedule intervals
  • Extends equipment life by avoiding unnecessary intervention and precisely timing repairs
  • Provides real-time visibility into asset health across the entire operation
  • Reduces unplanned downtime by detecting degradation early and allowing planned response
  • Builds a data history that improves decision-making over time
  • Enables better spare parts planning — you know what is needed before it fails

Disadvantages

  • Higher upfront cost for sensors, connectivity, and monitoring software
  • Requires technical knowledge to set up thresholds and interpret data correctly
  • Not all failure modes are detectable through monitoring — some are sudden and random
  • Sensors need their own maintenance — batteries, calibration, and connectivity checks
  • Initial baseline period needed before the system provides reliable alerts
  • Risk of alert fatigue if thresholds are not properly configured and maintained

When to Use Time-Based Maintenance

Time-based maintenance remains the right choice in several common scenarios. It is not outdated — it is simply better suited to certain types of equipment and requirements.

  • Regulatory or compliance tasks — inspections, testing, and certifications that must happen on a defined schedule regardless of equipment condition
  • Low-cost, replaceable assets — where the cost of monitoring exceeds the cost of the asset itself or its replacement
  • Consumables and wear items — filters, belts, and lubricants with well-known replacement intervals that are cheap to replace proactively
  • Equipment with consistent, predictable wear — assets that degrade at a steady rate under stable operating conditions
  • Simple maintenance tasks — lubrication, cleaning, and visual inspections that are quick, inexpensive, and provide value regardless of condition
  • Assets without monitorable parameters — some equipment does not have failure modes that lend themselves to sensor-based detection

For many organisations, time-based maintenance is the foundation of their preventive maintenance programme. It provides structure, ensures nothing is overlooked, and is accessible to teams at any level of technical maturity.

When to Use Condition-Based Maintenance

Condition-based maintenance delivers the most value when applied to assets where failure consequences are high and wear rates are variable or unpredictable.

  • Critical production equipment — where downtime directly impacts revenue, output, or customer commitments
  • High-value assets — where extending life and avoiding secondary damage justify the monitoring investment
  • Variable operating conditions — equipment that runs at different loads, speeds, or in different environments from day to day
  • Remote or hard-to-access equipment — where sending a technician for routine checks is impractical, expensive, or slow
  • Assets with unpredictable failure patterns — equipment that does not fail on a consistent, predictable schedule
  • Safety-critical systems — where failure could endanger personnel, the public, or the environment
  • Equipment with long lead-time spares — where early warning allows time to order parts that would otherwise cause extended downtime

Combining Both Approaches

In practice, the best maintenance programmes use both strategies together. The goal is to match the right strategy to each asset based on its criticality, failure behaviour, and the cost of monitoring versus the cost of failure.

A combined approach typically looks like this:

  • Critical assets — condition-based monitoring with IoT sensors, supplemented by essential scheduled inspections
  • Important assets — time-based preventive maintenance with periodic condition checks during scheduled tasks
  • Standard assets — time-based maintenance only, following manufacturer recommendations
  • Low-value assets — run-to-failure with reactive maintenance when they break
  • Compliance tasks — time-based scheduling to meet regulatory deadlines, regardless of asset criticality

This tiered approach ensures you invest monitoring resources where they deliver the greatest return, while still maintaining a solid preventive programme for everything else.

How Asset Criticality Guides the Decision

The first step in combining strategies is to classify your assets by criticality. A simple three-tier model works well for most organisations:

  • High criticality — failure causes major production loss, safety risk, or regulatory breach. These assets justify the investment in condition monitoring.
  • Medium criticality — failure causes moderate disruption but the operation can continue in a reduced capacity. Time-based preventive maintenance is usually sufficient.
  • Low criticality — failure has minimal impact and the asset is cheap to repair or replace. Run-to-failure or basic time-based maintenance is appropriate.

Once you have classified your assets, the strategy for each tier becomes clear. You are not choosing between CBM and TBM — you are choosing the right tool for each job.

How a CMMS Supports a Combined Strategy

Managing both time-based and condition-based maintenance in a single system is essential for a combined strategy to work. A CMMS platform lets you:

  • Schedule recurring preventive tasks based on time or usage intervals
  • Receive and act on condition-based alerts from IoT sensors
  • View all maintenance activity — scheduled and condition-triggered — in one place
  • Track asset history to evaluate whether each strategy is delivering results
  • Adjust schedules and thresholds based on real performance data
  • Assign, track, and report on all work orders regardless of how they were generated

Without a central system, time-based tasks and condition-based alerts exist in separate silos. This creates confusion about what has been done, what is pending, and what is being duplicated. A unified CMMS eliminates these problems.

The Role of Technology

Technology is the enabler that makes condition-based maintenance practical and cost-effective at scale. Several developments have lowered the barrier to adoption in recent years.

IoT Sensors

Wireless IoT sensors are now affordable, easy to install, and capable of measuring vibration, temperature, humidity, pressure, current, and more. Battery life has improved to multiple years in many cases, and wireless protocols like LoRaWAN allow sensors to operate across large facilities without extensive network infrastructure.

The cost per monitoring point has dropped significantly, making it feasible to monitor assets that would not have justified the investment five years ago.

Cloud Platforms

Cloud-based CMMS and monitoring platforms eliminate the need for on-premise servers and specialist IT support. Data from sensors is transmitted to the cloud, where it is stored, analysed, and made accessible from any device with an internet connection.

Cloud platforms also handle software updates, data backups, and security — reducing the IT burden on the maintenance team and the organisation.

Automated Alerting

Modern platforms allow you to define alert rules that automatically notify the right people or create work orders when thresholds are breached. This removes the need for someone to manually review dashboards and decide when to act, which is unreliable during busy periods or shift handovers.

Mobile Access

Technicians can receive alerts, view live sensor data, and complete work orders from their phone or tablet. This means faster response times and better information at the point of work. A technician can see the current vibration readings on a machine before they even walk over to inspect it.

Making the Transition

If your organisation currently relies entirely on time-based maintenance and wants to introduce condition-based monitoring, a gradual transition is the most practical path. Trying to implement CBM across all assets at once is expensive, complex, and unnecessary.

  • Step 1: Rank your assets by criticality and identify the top 5-10 where unplanned failure has the greatest impact
  • Step 2: For those assets, identify the primary failure modes and select appropriate monitoring sensors
  • Step 3: Install sensors and run them alongside your existing time-based schedule for a baseline period of two to four weeks
  • Step 4: Configure alert thresholds and integrate with your CMMS so alerts generate work orders
  • Step 5: Once condition monitoring is providing reliable data, review whether time-based tasks on those specific assets can be reduced, extended, or eliminated
  • Step 6: Measure results — downtime reduction, cost savings, false alarm rates — and use the evidence to expand monitoring to additional assets

The transition does not mean abandoning time-based maintenance entirely. It means being smarter about where you apply each strategy, using data to guide the decision rather than applying the same approach to every asset regardless of its needs.

Many organisations find that after introducing condition monitoring on critical assets, they can safely extend time-based intervals on those same assets — reducing preventive maintenance costs while actually improving reliability.

Key Takeaways

Neither time-based nor condition-based maintenance is universally better. Each has a clear role depending on the asset, the failure mode, and the consequences of downtime. Here are the essential points to remember:

  • Time-based maintenance is simple, low-cost, and effective for assets with predictable wear or compliance requirements
  • Condition-based maintenance reduces waste and catches problems earlier, but requires monitoring technology and configuration
  • The best maintenance programmes combine both strategies, matched to each asset's criticality and failure behaviour
  • IoT sensors and cloud-based CMMS have made condition-based maintenance more accessible and affordable than ever
  • A preventive maintenance platform that supports both scheduled and condition-triggered tasks is essential for a combined approach
  • Start with your most critical assets for condition monitoring and expand based on measurable results

Start by understanding your assets and their failure patterns. Apply the right strategy to each one, manage everything in a single system, and use data to continuously improve your maintenance outcomes.
