IoT condition monitoring uses connected sensors to continuously measure the health and performance of equipment in real time. Rather than relying on manual inspections or fixed maintenance schedules, organisations can track asset condition around the clock and respond to problems as they develop.

This guide covers the fundamentals of IoT condition monitoring — what it is, the types of monitoring available, how to implement it step by step, and how to connect it with your maintenance management system for maximum value.

What is Condition Monitoring?

Condition monitoring is the process of measuring specific parameters of equipment to detect changes that indicate developing faults. The concept is simple: healthy equipment behaves predictably, and deviations from that normal behaviour are early warning signs of failure.

Every piece of equipment has measurable characteristics that change as it degrades. A healthy bearing produces minimal vibration. A properly loaded motor draws a consistent current. A clean heat exchanger maintains a stable temperature differential. When these values change, something is wrong.

Traditionally, condition monitoring involved a technician visiting each asset with a handheld device to take readings. This approach works but has significant limitations:

  • Readings are only captured during inspection visits, not between them
  • Faults that develop rapidly can be missed entirely
  • Manual routes are time-consuming, especially across large or remote sites
  • Data is often recorded on paper or in spreadsheets, limiting analysis
  • The quality of readings depends on the operator's technique and consistency

IoT condition monitoring addresses all of these limitations by automating data collection and delivering it directly to your maintenance system.

How IoT Enables Condition Monitoring

IoT (Internet of Things) transforms condition monitoring from a periodic manual activity into a continuous, automated process. Low-cost wireless sensors are installed on equipment and transmit data over Wi-Fi, cellular, or other protocols to a central platform.

The result is a constant stream of equipment health data that is available to anyone who needs it — maintenance managers, technicians, reliability engineers, and operations staff — without anyone having to physically visit the machine.

The core components of an IoT condition monitoring system are:

  • Sensors — devices mounted on equipment that measure vibration, temperature, humidity, current, pressure, or flow
  • Connectivity — wireless protocols (Wi-Fi, LoRaWAN, cellular, Bluetooth) that transmit sensor data to a gateway or cloud
  • Data platform — cloud-based software that receives, stores, and analyses the data
  • Alerting — rules and thresholds that trigger notifications when readings are abnormal
  • CMMS integration — connection to your maintenance system to create work orders and track actions

Together, these components create a closed loop: sensors detect a problem, the system raises an alert, and the maintenance team responds through the CMMS before failure occurs.

Types of Condition Monitoring

Different equipment types fail in different ways. The monitoring technique you choose should match the most common failure modes for each asset. Here are the main types used in practice.

Vibration Monitoring

Vibration analysis is the most widely used condition monitoring technique for rotating machinery. Accelerometers mounted on bearings, motors, pumps, and fans measure vibration levels and frequency patterns.

What it detects:

  • Bearing wear and degradation
  • Shaft misalignment or imbalance
  • Looseness in mounting or foundations
  • Gear mesh problems
  • Belt or coupling defects

Vibration monitoring is the best starting point for most organisations because it covers a wide range of failure modes on common industrial equipment. Modern wireless vibration sensors are compact, battery-powered, and can be installed in minutes without specialist tools.

Temperature Monitoring

Temperature sensors and thermal imaging detect abnormal heat generation. This is particularly useful for electrical equipment, bearings, and processes where overheating is a primary failure mode.

What it detects:

  • Electrical connection faults and loose terminals
  • Overloaded circuits and components
  • Bearing friction and lubrication failure
  • Blocked ventilation or cooling system issues
  • Process temperature deviations

Temperature monitoring is especially valuable in electrical distribution systems, where a loose connection can generate increasing heat over weeks before causing a fire or outage. Continuous monitoring catches these issues far earlier than periodic thermal imaging surveys.

Power and Current Monitoring

Monitoring the electrical current, voltage, and power consumption of motors and other equipment reveals mechanical and electrical problems. A motor drawing higher-than-normal current may be working harder due to a mechanical issue downstream.

What it detects:

  • Increased mechanical load from worn components
  • Phase imbalance in three-phase systems
  • Insulation degradation in windings
  • Power quality issues affecting equipment life
  • Energy waste from inefficient operation

Current monitoring has the advantage of being non-invasive. Clamp-on current transformers can be installed around cables without disconnecting anything, making it practical for equipment that cannot be taken offline.

Flow and Pressure Monitoring

Sensors measuring flow rate and pressure in piping, hydraulic, and pneumatic systems detect degradation in pumps, valves, and system integrity.

What it detects:

  • Pump wear and impeller degradation
  • Valve leaks and blockages
  • Filter clogging
  • System leaks in compressed air or hydraulic circuits
  • Pressure relief valve issues

Flow and pressure data is particularly useful when correlated with other parameters. For example, rising motor current combined with falling pump output pressure is a strong indicator of impeller wear that warrants investigation.

Humidity and Environmental Monitoring

Environmental sensors track conditions that affect both equipment performance and product quality. This is important in facilities management, food processing, and clean manufacturing environments.

What it detects:

  • Excessive humidity that causes corrosion or mould
  • Temperature excursions in storage or production areas
  • Air quality issues affecting personnel or processes
  • Condensation risks in electrical enclosures

Implementation Steps

Implementing IoT condition monitoring does not require a massive upfront project. A phased approach lets you prove value quickly and expand based on results. Here is a practical step-by-step process.

1. Audit Your Assets

Review your asset register and identify equipment where unplanned failure has the highest impact. Consider production loss, repair cost, safety implications, and lead time for replacement parts. Rank assets by criticality to prioritise where to deploy sensors first.

Your maintenance history is a valuable resource here. Look at which assets have generated the most emergency work orders, the highest repair costs, and the most production downtime over the past one to two years.

2. Identify Failure Modes

For each priority asset, document how it typically fails. Review maintenance history, work orders, and failure records. Speak with technicians who know the equipment best — their practical experience is often more valuable than manufacturer documentation.

The failure mode determines which monitoring technique to apply. A motor with recurring bearing failures needs vibration monitoring. An electrical panel with a history of overheating needs temperature monitoring. Match the sensor to the failure, not the other way around.

3. Select Sensors

Choose sensors that match your identified failure modes and operating environment. Key factors to consider:

  • Measurement type — vibration, temperature, current, pressure, or multi-parameter
  • Mounting method — magnetic, adhesive, bolt-on, or clamp depending on the surface
  • Wireless protocol — Wi-Fi, LoRaWAN, cellular, or Bluetooth depending on site infrastructure and range
  • Power source — battery life, mains powered, or energy harvesting for long-term deployment
  • Environmental rating — IP rating for dust, moisture, and temperature extremes in your operating environment
  • Sampling frequency — how often data points are captured, based on how fast faults typically develop

If you are unsure about sensor selection, look for a provider that offers guidance as part of their service. The right sensor in the wrong location is as unhelpful as no sensor at all.

4. Install and Establish Baselines

Mount sensors on selected assets and allow the system to collect data for two to four weeks during normal operation. This baseline period establishes what "healthy" looks like for each asset.

Important considerations for the baseline period:

  • Ensure the equipment is running under typical conditions, not during startup or shutdown
  • Include different operating modes if the equipment runs at varying loads or speeds
  • Verify sensor connectivity is reliable throughout the baseline period
  • Check that data is being received and stored correctly in the platform

5. Configure Alerts and Thresholds

Set alert levels based on your baseline data, manufacturer specifications, and industry standards. Most systems support multiple alert tiers:

  • Warning — readings are elevated but the asset can continue operating while you plan a response
  • Critical — readings indicate imminent failure; immediate attention required

Start with conservative thresholds and refine them over time as you build experience with your specific equipment. It is better to start slightly loose and tighten than to start too tight and generate alert fatigue from day one.

6. Connect to Your CMMS

The real value of condition monitoring is realised when alerts flow directly into your maintenance management system. This means alerts automatically generate work orders, assigned to the right technician, with the relevant asset details already attached.

Without CMMS integration, alerts exist in a separate system and require manual action to turn into maintenance tasks. This introduces delays and increases the chance that alerts are overlooked during busy periods.

The ideal setup is a platform where IoT monitoring and CMMS are part of the same system, eliminating the need for third-party integration, middleware, or manual data transfer.

7. Review and Expand

After three to six months, review the results. Measure how many alerts led to genuine interventions, how much downtime was avoided, and whether thresholds need adjustment. Use these insights to expand monitoring to additional assets.

Document what worked and what did not. Share results with the maintenance team and wider organisation to build support for the programme. Tangible results — prevented failures, saved hours, reduced costs — are the most effective way to secure ongoing investment.

Integrating IoT Monitoring with CMMS

CMMS integration is the step that turns monitoring data into maintenance action. Without it, you have information but no structured process to act on it. A well-integrated system provides:

  • Automatic work order creation when alerts are triggered
  • Full asset history combining sensor data with maintenance records
  • Dashboard visibility of asset health across the entire operation
  • Reporting that links condition data to maintenance costs and downtime
  • Mobile access so technicians can view live sensor data on site
  • Audit trail showing when alerts were raised, acknowledged, and resolved

Platforms that combine IoT and CMMS natively — rather than requiring third-party integration — provide the simplest and most reliable path to value. Data flows directly from sensor to work order without middleware, API configuration, or sync delays.

This native integration also ensures that asset records are automatically enriched with condition data, giving you a complete picture of each asset's health, history, and maintenance activity in one place.

Best Practices

Organisations that get the most from IoT condition monitoring follow a few consistent principles.

Start Small and Focused

Begin with five to ten critical assets rather than trying to monitor everything at once. This keeps the project manageable, builds internal expertise, and delivers measurable results that justify further investment. A focused start also lets you learn what works in your specific environment before scaling up.

Involve Your Maintenance Team Early

Technicians understand equipment behaviour better than anyone. Involve them in selecting assets, defining thresholds, and interpreting data. Their buy-in is essential for adoption and long-term success. A monitoring programme that the maintenance team does not trust or understand will not deliver results.

Focus on Actionable Alerts

Avoid alert fatigue by ensuring every notification is meaningful and actionable. Too many false alarms cause teams to ignore alerts entirely. Tune thresholds based on real operating data and refine them over time. Every alert should have a clear next step — if it does not, the threshold needs adjusting.

Use Data to Improve, Not Just React

Condition data is most valuable when it informs long-term decisions — not just immediate repairs. Look for patterns across assets, track degradation rates, and use the data to optimise maintenance intervals, inform capital replacement plans, and identify systemic issues such as recurring installation problems or environmental factors.

Keep Sensor Infrastructure Maintained

Sensors are physical devices that need occasional attention. Check battery levels, verify wireless connectivity, and confirm sensors remain securely mounted. A sensor that stops reporting is worse than no sensor at all because it creates a false sense of security. Include sensor health checks in your regular maintenance rounds.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

Learning from others' mistakes accelerates your success. Here are the pitfalls that most commonly undermine IoT condition monitoring programmes.

  • Monitoring too many assets too quickly — start with critical equipment and expand gradually based on results
  • Setting thresholds too tight — this generates excessive false alarms and erodes team trust in the system
  • Collecting data without a plan to act on it — data is only valuable if it drives maintenance decisions
  • Ignoring environmental factors — temperature, humidity, and vibration from nearby equipment can affect sensor readings
  • Skipping CMMS integration — without a connection to your maintenance system, alerts become just another inbox to check
  • Neglecting training — maintenance teams need to understand what the data means and how to respond to different alert types
  • Poor sensor placement — mounting a vibration sensor on a flexible bracket rather than solid structure will produce unreliable readings
  • Not reviewing results — without periodic review, thresholds become stale and the programme loses effectiveness

What to Look for in an IoT Monitoring Platform

If you are evaluating platforms for IoT condition monitoring, consider the following criteria to find the best fit for your organisation.

  • Native CMMS integration — the platform should connect monitoring data directly to work orders and asset records without third-party tools
  • Included hardware — a complete solution with sensors, gateways, and connectivity reduces implementation effort and finger-pointing between vendors
  • Ease of setup — sensors should be quick to install without specialist knowledge or expensive commissioning services
  • Flexible alerting — configurable thresholds with warning and critical tiers, plus the ability to refine over time
  • Mobile access — technicians should be able to view live data and respond to alerts from any device, anywhere
  • Scalability — the platform should grow with your monitoring programme without per-sensor licensing complexity
  • Support — look for a vendor that provides guidance on sensor selection, placement, and threshold configuration as part of the service
  • Data retention — long-term data storage is essential for trend analysis and identifying gradual degradation over months or years

Summary

IoT condition monitoring gives maintenance teams continuous visibility into equipment health. By combining the right sensors with a connected CMMS platform, organisations can detect problems early, respond before failures occur, and make data-driven decisions that improve reliability and reduce costs.

Start with your most critical assets, choose sensors that match your failure modes, and ensure alerts flow directly into your maintenance workflow. The technology is proven, the hardware is affordable, and the payback is measurable.

The organisations that succeed with condition monitoring are the ones that treat it not as a technology project but as a maintenance improvement programme — with the right people, processes, and tools working together.
