Introduction

Maintenance strategies generally fall into two categories: reactive and preventive maintenance.

Understanding the difference is key to improving efficiency, reducing downtime, and lowering maintenance costs.

In this guide, we explain both approaches, compare them, and help you decide which is right for your organisation.

What is Reactive Maintenance?

Reactive maintenance means fixing equipment only after it fails.

Also known as "run-to-failure" or "breakdown maintenance", this approach involves:

  • No planned maintenance activities
  • Repairs only when equipment breaks down
  • Unplanned work orders and emergency responses

While simple, reactive maintenance often leads to higher costs and more disruption.

Common Problems with Reactive Maintenance

  • Unexpected equipment failures
  • Unplanned downtime affecting production
  • Higher repair costs (overtime, emergency parts)
  • Shortened asset lifespan
  • Safety risks from equipment failure

What is Preventive Maintenance?

Preventive maintenance involves scheduled servicing performed at regular intervals to prevent failures before they occur.

This includes:

  • Routine inspections and checks
  • Scheduled servicing and part replacements
  • Automated work order generation
  • Condition monitoring and tracking

Preventive maintenance aims to keep equipment in good condition and avoid unexpected breakdowns.

Benefits of Preventive Maintenance

  • Reduced unplanned downtime
  • Lower long-term maintenance costs
  • Extended asset lifespan
  • Improved safety and compliance
  • Better planning and resource allocation

Key Differences

Factor Reactive Maintenance Preventive Maintenance
Planning Unplanned Planned and scheduled
Downtime Higher and unpredictable Lower and controlled

When Reactive Maintenance Makes Sense

Reactive maintenance is not always wrong. It may be appropriate for:

  • Non-critical equipment with low failure impact
  • Assets that are inexpensive to replace
  • Equipment with unpredictable failure patterns

However, relying solely on reactive maintenance is rarely the best strategy.

When Preventive Maintenance is Essential

Preventive maintenance should be used for:

  • Critical production equipment
  • High-value assets
  • Equipment where failure causes safety risks
  • Assets with known wear patterns
  • Compliance-related inspections

The 80/20 Rule

Most effective maintenance organisations aim for a ratio of approximately:

  • 80% planned (preventive) maintenance
  • 20% reactive maintenance

This balance ensures that most work is planned and controlled, while still allowing for unexpected issues.

How to Transition from Reactive to Preventive

Making the shift doesn't have to be complex:

Step 1: Identify Critical Assets

Start with the equipment that causes the most disruption when it fails.

Step 2: Define Maintenance Tasks

Create maintenance checklists for each asset with clear intervals.

Step 3: Set Up Scheduling

Use preventive maintenance software to automate recurring tasks.

Step 4: Track Everything

Record all maintenance activity in a CMMS to build history and identify patterns.

Step 5: Review and Improve

Use maintenance KPIs to measure progress and continuously optimise.

The Role of Technology

Technology makes the transition easier:

  • CMMS — centralises all maintenance operations
  • Mobile access — allows technicians to update tasks on the shop floor
  • IoT sensors — enable condition-based and predictive maintenance
  • Automated scheduling — ensures nothing is missed

Conclusion

Moving from reactive to preventive maintenance is one of the most impactful improvements an organisation can make.

It reduces downtime, lowers costs, extends asset lifespan, and gives maintenance teams structured, manageable workflows.

The key is to start with critical assets, use the right tools, and build from there.
