Every maintenance team operates somewhere on the spectrum between purely reactive and fully preventive. Most sit closer to the reactive end than they would like.

The question is not whether reactive maintenance is bad and preventive is good. Both have a place. The question is which strategy — or mix of strategies — makes the most sense for your assets, your budget, and your operational goals.

This guide breaks down both approaches, compares them head to head, and helps you decide where to focus your effort.

What Is Reactive Maintenance?

Reactive maintenance — also called run-to-failure, breakdown maintenance, or corrective maintenance — means you fix equipment after it breaks. No scheduled inspections, no planned servicing. You wait for a failure, then respond.

This approach involves:

  • No scheduled maintenance activities
  • Repairs triggered only by equipment failure
  • Emergency work orders as the primary workflow
  • Technicians responding to breakdowns as they happen
  • Spare parts ordered after the failure occurs

Reactive maintenance is the default state for many organisations. It requires no upfront planning and feels simpler to manage — until the breakdowns start piling up.

What Is Preventive Maintenance?

Preventive maintenance — also called planned maintenance or scheduled maintenance — means you service equipment at regular intervals to prevent failures before they happen. It is proactive rather than reactive.

This approach involves:

  • Scheduled inspections at defined intervals
  • Routine servicing tasks (lubrication, filter changes, calibration)
  • Planned work orders generated automatically
  • Replacement of wear parts before they fail
  • Data-driven decisions on maintenance intervals

Preventive maintenance requires upfront investment in planning, but it pays for itself through reduced downtime, lower repair costs, and longer asset life.

Pros and Cons of Reactive Maintenance

Advantages

  • Zero planning overhead — no schedules to manage, no checklists to build
  • Lower short-term cost — you do not spend money until something breaks
  • Simple to manage — no CMMS required, no training on PM processes
  • No risk of over-maintenance — you never replace a part that still has life left

Disadvantages

  • Unpredictable downtime — failures happen at the worst possible time
  • Higher total cost — emergency repairs cost 3-9 times more than planned maintenance
  • Secondary damage — one failure often causes damage to adjacent components
  • Safety hazards — unexpected failures put workers at risk
  • Shorter asset life — running equipment to failure accelerates wear across the whole machine
  • Stressed teams — constant firefighting leads to burnout and high turnover
  • Compliance gaps — no documented maintenance history for auditors

Pros and Cons of Preventive Maintenance

Advantages

  • Reduced unplanned downtime — studies show PM programs cut unplanned downtime by 30-50%
  • Lower total maintenance cost — planned work is significantly cheaper than emergency repairs
  • Longer equipment life — regular servicing extends useful life by 20-40%
  • Improved safety — routine inspections catch hazards before they cause injuries
  • Better resource planning — scheduled work lets you plan labour, parts, and downtime windows
  • Compliance ready — complete maintenance records for audits and regulatory requirements
  • Actionable data — trends and patterns guide smarter maintenance decisions

Disadvantages

  • Setup time — building checklists, schedules, and asset records takes effort
  • Potential over-maintenance — parts may be replaced before they are worn out
  • Ongoing discipline required — PM only works if the team follows through consistently
  • Initial cost — implementing a CMMS and training staff requires upfront investment

When to Use Reactive Maintenance

Reactive maintenance is not always the wrong choice. For certain assets, it can be the most cost-effective approach. The key is being deliberate about where you apply it.

Reactive maintenance makes sense when:

  • The asset is non-critical — its failure does not stop production or create safety hazards
  • Replacement is cheap and fast — it costs less to replace than to maintain (e.g., light bulbs, small pumps)
  • Failure is unpredictable — some components fail randomly regardless of maintenance
  • Redundancy exists — backup systems can take over immediately when the primary fails
  • The asset is near end of life — investing in PM for equipment being replaced soon offers poor return

The danger is applying this logic to assets where it does not fit. A cheap pump that feeds a critical production line is not a good candidate for run-to-failure, even if the pump itself is inexpensive.

When to Use Preventive Maintenance

Preventive maintenance should be the default strategy for most of your asset base. The return on investment is clear and well-documented across industries.

Preventive maintenance is essential when:

  • The asset is critical to operations — its failure stops production or impacts revenue
  • Failure creates safety risks — pressure vessels, electrical systems, lifting equipment
  • Regulatory compliance is required — many industries mandate documented maintenance programs
  • The asset is expensive to repair or replace — prevention is always cheaper than major rebuild
  • Failure patterns are predictable — wear-based failures respond well to scheduled maintenance
  • Downtime is costly — every hour of unplanned downtime has a significant financial impact

The 80/20 Rule

Most successful maintenance operations do not choose one strategy exclusively. They use a blend, often following what is called the 80/20 rule.

The target is 80% planned maintenance (preventive) and 20% reactive maintenance. This ratio acknowledges that some reactive work is unavoidable — things do break unexpectedly — but it ensures the vast majority of your maintenance effort is proactive and controlled.

World-class maintenance organisations operate above 90% planned work. If your ratio is currently 50/50 or worse, there is significant room for improvement.

Transitioning from Reactive to Preventive

Shifting from a reactive to a preventive approach does not happen overnight. It requires a structured transition plan. Here is how to do it.

Step 1: Classify Your Assets by Criticality

Not every asset needs the same level of attention. Rank your assets by their impact on production, safety, and cost. Focus your PM program on the most critical assets first.

Step 2: Start with Quick Wins

Identify assets that break frequently and have predictable failure modes. These are the easiest to move from reactive to preventive. A successful early result builds momentum for the broader rollout.

Step 3: Build Your Asset Register

Document every asset: location, manufacturer, model, serial number, installation date, and current condition. You cannot manage what you have not catalogued. A CMMS makes this process significantly easier.

Step 4: Create PM Schedules

Use manufacturer recommendations, failure history, and team knowledge to define maintenance intervals. Start conservative — you can always extend intervals once you have data showing the asset is performing well.

Step 5: Implement a CMMS

A CMMS automates scheduling, tracks completion, and provides the reporting you need to measure progress. It is the backbone of any serious preventive maintenance program. Without it, you are relying on spreadsheets and memory.

Step 6: Train and Engage Your Team

Your maintenance team needs to understand why the change is happening and how it benefits them. A PM program reduces emergency callouts, overtime, and stress. Frame the transition as an improvement for the team, not just for management.

Step 7: Measure and Adjust

Track your planned vs unplanned maintenance ratio, PM compliance rate, and downtime metrics. Review monthly. Celebrate improvements. Adjust schedules based on real performance data.

Real-World Impact

The numbers consistently favour preventive maintenance for critical assets. Here is what the research shows.

  • Reactive maintenance costs 3-9 times more per incident than planned maintenance
  • Organisations with PM programs see 25-30% lower total maintenance costs
  • Preventive maintenance reduces unplanned downtime by 30-50%
  • Equipment lifespan increases by 20-40% with regular preventive care
  • Best-in-class organisations maintain a 90%+ planned maintenance ratio
  • Unplanned downtime costs industrial manufacturers an estimated $50 billion annually

The transition from reactive to preventive takes time and commitment. But the financial, safety, and operational benefits are well established. Start with your most critical assets, build good habits, and expand from there.

Making the Right Choice

The answer to "reactive or preventive?" is almost always "both, in the right proportions." Use criticality analysis to decide which assets get preventive maintenance and which can safely run to failure.

For most operations, the path forward is clear: reduce reactive work, increase planned maintenance, and use a CMMS to manage the transition. The organisations that do this consistently outperform those that stay in firefighting mode.

The best time to start a preventive maintenance program was years ago. The second best time is now.
