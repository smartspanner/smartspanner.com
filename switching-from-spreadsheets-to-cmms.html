Your spreadsheet worked. Until it didn't.

Most manufacturers don't start with a CMMS. They start with a spreadsheet, and for a while, it is fine. Then a machine goes down and nobody remembers when it was last serviced. A technician who "just knew" how everything worked leaves the company. An auditor asks for maintenance records, and someone spends two days digging through email attachments and printed sheets to prove the work actually happened.

None of this means you did anything wrong. It means you have outgrown the tool, not the process.

Switching from spreadsheets to a CMMS should not mean throwing away the maintenance knowledge your team has already built. It should mean putting that knowledge somewhere safer, easier to use, and easier to prove.

Why manufacturers put this off

The idea of switching systems sounds like a project: weeks of setup, retraining your whole team, and disrupting a maintenance schedule that already works well enough. That fear is reasonable. It is also based on how CMMS software used to work, not how it works now.

You are not replacing your maintenance program. You are not starting over. You are taking what already exists in your spreadsheet, including your equipment list, schedules, and history, and giving it a home that does not depend on one file, one person, or one laptop.

What actually changes

The work does not become more complicated. The record keeping becomes automatic.

Before

A technician finishes a job and maybe updates the spreadsheet later, if they remember. Maintenance history lives in someone's head or a folder of old service sheets. When something breaks, nobody is sure if it was serviced last month or last year.

After

Technicians get told what to do and when, with no memory required. Every completed job is logged automatically and tied to the asset. When a machine fails, you can see exactly what was done, by whom, and when.

Before

An audit or compliance request means hours of searching for proof that maintenance actually happened, often across email attachments, paper sheets, and old versions of shared files.

After

A compliance report exists because the system built it as work got done, not because someone scrambled to reconstruct it after the request arrived.

The practical gains

The biggest improvement is not having a more impressive system. It is removing the small gaps that spreadsheets leave behind.

Technicians know what is due

Planned work is assigned and visible on desktop or mobile, so jobs do not depend on someone remembering to check the spreadsheet.

Asset history stays with the asset

Breakdowns, services, photos, parts, and labour are stored against the equipment record, not scattered across folders.

Compliance evidence builds itself

Completed work creates the record as your team goes, making audits and customer requests easier to answer.

Start with the first useful workflow

You do not need to model every asset and maintenance process before the system becomes useful. Start with the equipment and jobs the team needs now, run that workflow and add more depth as the record develops.

AMES Goldsmith UK began by logging jobs, then added expected durations, due dates, engineer utilisation, KPI reporting, preventive maintenance and statutory inspections. The first workflow was useful before the wider rollout was complete.
